18  Class 18: Manufactured Solutions and Galerkin Orthogonality

Where the exact solution comes from, and why the estimate describes our answer

The discussion in Class 17 did not cover two important parts of convergence analysis. First, we spent a whole class measuring how far \(u^h\) is from \(u\), without ever saying where \(u\) is supposed to come from. On a real problem we don’t actually have \(u\), and that is why we are running the solver in the first place. The second is more theoretical: the error estimate we wrote down is a statement about polynomials, and we never justified using it to describe the answer our solver actually produced. Today will cover both of these issues.

18.1 Manufacturing a solution

18.1.1 Running the problem backwards

Normally you are handed \(f\), \(\mathcal{g}\) and \(\mathcal{h}\), and you go looking for \(u\). That is the hard direction, and in general there is no formula at the end of it.

The trick is to run the arrow the other way. Pick the answer first, then work out which problem it solves. Choosing \(u\) is free — you can write down any function you like — and once you have it, finding the data that produces it is just differentiation, which is easy.

For the problem we have been solving all semester,

\[ u_{,xx} + f = 0 \ \text{ on } \Omega = (0,L), \qquad u(L) = \mathcal{g}, \qquad -u_{,x}(0) = \mathcal{h}, \]

the recipe is three steps:

  1. Choose any \(u\) you like, as long as it is smooth enough to differentiate twice.
  2. Differentiate it twice and set \(f = -u_{,xx}\).
  3. Evaluate at the two ends: \(\mathcal{g} = u(L)\) and \(\mathcal{h} = -u_{,x}(0)\).

Now feed \(f\), \(\mathcal{g}\) and \(\mathcal{h}\) to your solver. It has no idea anything unusual has happened — it is solving an ordinary boundary value problem. But you know the exact answer, so you can subtract it from \(u^h\) and measure the error for real.

This is called a manufactured solution, and it is how essentially all finite element software is verified.

18.1.2 A worked example

Take

\[ u(x) = \sin(\pi x) \qquad \text{on } \Omega = (0,1) . \]

Differentiate twice:

\[ u_{,x} = \pi \cos(\pi x), \qquad u_{,xx} = -\pi^2 \sin(\pi x), \]

so the forcing that produces this solution is

\[ f = -u_{,xx} = \pi^2 \sin(\pi x) . \]

The boundary data comes straight from evaluating:

\[ \mathcal{g} = u(1) = \sin(\pi) = 0, \qquad \mathcal{h} = -u_{,x}(0) = -\pi . \]

That is the whole derivation. Hand \(f = \pi^2\sin(\pi x)\), \(\mathcal{g} = 0\) and \(\mathcal{h} = -\pi\) to the solver, run the convergence study from Class 17, and the measured rates come out as

\(p\) \(H^0\) rate \(H^1\) rate predicted
1 2.00 1.00 2, 1
2 3.00 2.00 3, 2
3 3.98 3.00 4, 3

exactly the \(\beta = p + 1 - s\) the estimate promised.

For \(p=5\), notice that the rate flattens or even slightly regresses for error around \(10^{-12}\). This does not indicate that the solver is incorrect here. Instead, it is an artifact of using double-precision arithmetic. We cannot typically represent solutions more accurately than machine precision, and so the benefits for refinement in the \(L^2\) norm cannot be adequately measured. However, this is not to say that the solution does not improve: by looking at the \(H^1\) norm you can see that error continues to decrease over the depicted number of elements with refinement even after error bottoms out in the \(L^2\) norm.

18.1.3 What this does and does not tell you

A manufactured solution is a verification tool: it answers did I implement the method correctly? If your rates come out at the predicted \(\beta\), your basis functions, your Jacobians, your assembly and your boundary conditions are almost certainly all right. Most coding mistakes break the rate, not just the constant, because they break the method rather than merely making it less accurate.

What it does not tell you is whether you solved the right problem (i.e. used the correct input). The physics, the geometry, the material data and the boundary conditions you chose are all still your responsibility. Verification asks whether you solved the equations right; it does not ask whether you had the right equations.

One more caution. You are free to pick any \(u\), which means you are also free to pick one that makes the test easier than it should be. If you choose a \(u\) that your basis can represent exactly (e.g. a straight line with \(p = 1\)), the error is zero at every refinement and you don’t see the convergence rates play out. Pick something your space genuinely has to approximate.

18.2 Why the estimate describes our solution

Here is the gap Class 17 left open. The a-priori estimate

\[ \|e\|_{H^s(\Omega)} \leq C h^{\beta} \|u\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)} \]

comes from approximation theory. It says: given a mesh and a polynomial degree, here is how close we can guarantee the best possible function in that space gets to your arbitrary choice of \(u\). The rest of the material for this class aims to help us understand where this estimate came from.

18.2.1 Step one: the error is invisible to the approximation space

Write the left side of the weak form as

\[ a(w, v) := \int_\Omega w_{,x}\, v_{,x} \, dx .\]

or in multiple dimensions as

\[ a(w, v) := \int_\Omega w_{,i}\, \kappa_{ij} v_{,j} \, d\Omega .\]

This is short notation for exactly what your stiffness matrix computes, since \(K_{AB} = a(N_A, N_B)\).

Now put the two problems side by side. The exact solution satisfies the weak form against every test function \(w\in \mathcal{V}\):

\[ a(u, w) = L(w) \qquad \text{for all } w \in \mathcal{V} ,\]

where \(L(w)\) accounts for the forcing and boundary terms. The computed finite element solution satisfies the same equation, but only against the functions we actually built:

\[ a(u^h, w^h) = L(w^h) \qquad \text{for all } w^h \in \mathcal{V}^h .\]

Now, recall that \(\mathcal{V}^h \subset \mathcal{V}\), meaning that every function in \(\mathcal{V}^h\) is also in \(\mathcal{V}\). The basis functions we use are legitimate test functions, but happen to be just a small subset of them. So the first equation is true for \(w = w^h\) as well, and we may subtract the second equation from it:

\[ \boxed{\; a\!\left(u - u^h,\; w^h\right) = 0 \qquad \text{for all } w^h \in \mathcal{V}^h \;}\]

This is Galerkin orthogonality. If you look at \(a(w,v)\) as an inner product between two functions, it says that \(u-u^h\) is orthogonal to \(w^h\) under this physics-based inner product. In other words: measured in this energy inner product, the error, \(e= u-u^h\), is orthogonal to everything our approximation space can represent. The space cannot see its own error. If it could, the method would have used that information and done better.

18.2.2 Step two: so \(u^h\) is the best the space can do

Using the above energy inner product, define the energy norm by

\[ \|v\|_E := \sqrt{a(v,v)} .\]

For our problem this is just the size of a function’s derivative, so it is the \(H^1\) part of the error you have already been computing, which in general is weighted by the (positive definite) thermal conductivity matrix.

Now take any \(w^h\) in the space and split the difference between \(u\) and it into two pieces:

\[ u - w^h = \underbrace{\left(u - u^h\right)}_{\text{the error}} + \underbrace{\left(u^h - w^h\right)}_{\text{lives in } \mathcal{V}^h} .\]

Measure the size of both sides in the energy norm. Because \(a(\cdot,\cdot)\) is symmetric, this expands the way a squared length does, and the cross term is \(a(u - u^h,\, u^h - w^h)\) — which is zero, by Galerkin orthogonality, since the second slot lives in \(\mathcal{V}^h\). What is left is

\[ \left\|u - w^h\right\|_E^2 = \left\|u - u^h\right\|_E^2 + \left\|u^h - w^h\right\|_E^2 .\]

That is the Pythagorean theorem, with \(a(\cdot,\cdot)\) playing the role of the dot product. The last term cannot be negative, so dropping it and rearranging gives

\[ \boxed{\; \left\|u - u^h\right\|_E \;\leq\; \left\|u - w^h\right\|_E \qquad \text{for every } w^h \in \mathcal{V}^h \;}\]

The finite element solution is the best approximation in its space, measured in the energy norm. No other combination of your basis functions gets closer. This result is called Céa’s lemma.

It also clarifies what we have indicated repeatedly, that the finite element method is not an interpolation but is instead a best fit or projection. Under our physics-informed energy inner product, the finite element method finds the best approximation/solution to the real solution in a similar sense that projecting a 2D point onto the \(x\)-axis finds the closest point on the 1D line.

18.2.4 Why this is not interpolation

Look again at the widget in Section 18.1.2, at \(p=1\) with a few elements. The finite element curve passes exactly through the exact solution at every node. It is very tempting to conclude that the method works by interpolation — that it reads off \(u\) at the nodes and joins the dots.

It does not, and one dimension is misleading. Nodal exactness in 1D for the Poisson equation is a genuine theorem, but it is special to one dimension, and it stops being true in higher dimensions.

Below is the same manufactured problem in two dimensions, \(u = \sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)\) on the unit square, solved with code that will be developed in Project 2. Both panels are a slice along \(y = \tfrac{1}{2}\), where the exact solution is just \(\sin(\pi x)\) again. The grey dashed line is \(u\), the thin blue line joins the dots through \(u\) at the nodes, and the heavy red line is what the solver actually returns.

A slice at y = ½ through the finite element solution of u = sin(πx) sin(πy) on [0, 1]². On the coarse mesh the finite element answer overshoots the exact solution by 23% at the center, and it is still 5.3% high after one refinement. An interpolant cannot do that: it is pinned to u at every node by construction. What you are looking at is the method choosing the closest point in its space measured in the energy norm, which is a statement about slopes over the whole domain, not about values at points. In one dimension that choice happens to land on the nodal values. In two it does not, and the projection shows itself.

18.2.5 The whole argument in three lines

The error estimate of Class 17 is the result of Galerkin orthogonality to get Céa’s lemma and then composing this with another lemma about polynomial interpolation. Here, Galerkin Orthogonality allows us to prove Céa’s lemma, and Céa’s lemma lets us swap \(u^h\) for any function we like out of the space. The polynomial function that interpolates the actual solution is one such function that could be used. The Bramble–Hilbert lemma says how well polynomials can interpolate a smooth function, with the interpolant written as \(\mathcal{I}_h u\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\|u - u^h\right\|_E &= \min_{w^h \in \mathcal{V}^h} \left\|u - w^h\right\|_E && \text{(Céa's lemma)} \\[2pt] &\leq \left\|u - \mathcal{I}_h u\right\|_E && \text{(pick the interpolant)} \\[2pt] &\leq C \, h^{\,p} \, \left\|u\right\|_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)} && \text{(Bramble–Hilbert)} \end{aligned} \]

The convergence rate \(h^p\) and the norm \(\|u\|_{H^{p+1}}\) both appear only on the last line, and could be altered slightly for less smooth solutions. The constant \(C\) also comes as a result of this interpolation theory lemma in the last line. Here, \(C\) measures how well degree-\(p\) polynomials fit a smooth function on an element.

18.2.6 A reading of this worth carrying forward

Saying \(u^h\) is the closest point is the same as saying \(u^h\) minimizes the error over everything in the space:

\[ \left\|u - u^h\right\|_E = \min_{w^h \in \mathcal{V}^h} \left\|u - w^h\right\|_E .\]

Keep that word minimizes in mind. There are two different minimization statements in this course and it is worth not confusing them. This one is about the approximation: of all the answers we could have built, we got the closest. The other one, in Class 23, is about the physics: the true solution \(u\) is the one that minimizes the total potential energy of the system over all admissible (test) functions, not just the ones our mesh can build.

18.3 When a measured rate is not what you expected

Sometime you may run a convergence study and get a number that is not \(\beta\). This still may mean that nothing is wrong if it is the result of one of the following cases.

You are not refined enough yet. The estimate is asymptotic — it describes behavior as \(h \to 0\). On coarse meshes the measured rate typically creeps up on \(\beta\) from below rather than hitting it. Seeing 1.94 where you expected 2.00 is normal, and is often resolved after one more level of refinement.

You have hit machine precision. This one surprises people. At high polynomial degree the error drops so fast that after two or three refinements it reaches roundoff, around \(10^{-16}\), and it cannot get any smaller. The slope then flattens toward zero, and a plot that was beautifully straight suddenly bends. The method has not stopped working, but double precision arithmetic limits continuing.

Your quadrature is not good enough for the error integral. The error \(e = u - u^h\) is not a polynomial, since \(u\) generally is not, so the rule that integrates your stiffness matrix exactly will not integrate the error norm exactly. Use more points than you think you need, and confirm that adding more does not change the answer.
