20  Class 20: Quadrature in 2D, and Integrating Over a Face

The tensor product, and why a boundary integral uses a different Jacobian

Two things stand between the one-dimensional code and a working multidimensional one, and both are about integration. The first is easy and is mostly bookkeeping: a quadrature rule on the parent square is built from the rule on the parent interval, by multiplying. The second is not bookkeeping at all: integrating over the boundary of an element needs a different Jacobian from the one you use inside it.

20.1 The 2D rule is the 1D rule, twice

A Gauss rule on \([-1,1]\) is a list of points \(\xi_g\) and weights \(w_g\) chosen so that

\[ \int_{-1}^{1} f(\xi)\,d\xi \;\approx\; \sum_{g} w_g\, f(\xi_g) \]

is exact whenever \(f\) is a polynomial of degree \(2n-1\) or less. On the parent square we want the same thing in two variables, and because the square is a product of two intervals, we get it for free:

\[ \int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\int_{-1}^{1} f(\xi,\eta)\;d\xi\,d\eta \;\approx\; \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \underbrace{w_i\,w_j}_{\text{one weight}}\; f\big(\underbrace{\xi_i,\;\eta_j}_{\text{one point}}\big) . \]

Every 2D point is a pair of 1D points, and its weight is the product of the two 1D weights. Nothing is re-derived. Move the sliders and watch the grid come from the two rules on the edges.

20.2 Integrating over a face

Everything so far has been about integrating over the inside of an element, where the Jacobian is \(\det(DF)\) and it converts \(d\xi\,d\eta\) into \(d\Omega\). A Neumann term is not that integral. It runs over one face (or more faces) of the element,

\[ \int_{\Gamma_h^e} N_A\, h \; d\Gamma , \]

and a face is a curve, not an area. This requires two additional changes.

A face is a one-dimensional parent domain. Fix one parent coordinate at \(\pm 1\) and the other one still runs over \([-1,1]\). So a face integral is a 1D quadrature operation.

You only use part of the deformation gradient. Along a face where \(\eta\) is held fixed, the curve is traced only by \(\xi\), so its tangent is

\[ \underline{t} = \frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial \xi} \]

The thing that converts \(d\xi\) into arc length \(d\Gamma\) is the length of that tangent,

\[ \boxed{\;d\Gamma = \left\| \underline{t} \right\| d\xi \;} \]

which is not \(\det(DF)\).

Similarly, along a face where \(\xi\) is held fixed, the curve is traced only by \(\eta\), so its tangent is

\[ \underline{t} = \frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial \eta} \]

and the arclength is

\[ \boxed{\;d\Gamma = \left\| \underline{t} \right\| d\eta. \;} \]

Consequently, integration over a face which, for instance has \(\xi=1\) can be approximated using quadrature as

\[ \int_{\Gamma_h^e} N_A(1,\eta)\, h\big(x(1,\eta)) \; d\Gamma \approx \sum_q^{n_g} w_g N_A(1,\eta_g) h\big(x(1,\eta_g)) \big|\big|\underline{t}\big(x(1,\eta_g)\big)\big|\big|. \]

Similarly, integration over a face which, for instance, has \(\eta=-1\) can be approximated as

\[ \int_{\Gamma_h^e} N_A(\xi,-1)\, h\big(x(\xi,-1)) \; d\Gamma \approx \sum_q^{n_g} w_g N_A(\xi_g,-1) h\big(x(\xi_g,-1)) \big|\big|\underline{t}\big(x(\xi_g,-1)\big)\big|\big|. \]

20.2.1 Where that length comes from

The boxed formula is not a definition. It falls out of asking one question: if I take a small step along the face in the parent domain, how far have I actually moved in the physical element?

Hold \(\eta\) fixed and step from \(\xi\) to \(\xi + \Delta\xi\). A Taylor expansion of the mapping gives

\[ \underline{x}(\xi + \Delta\xi,\, \eta) \;\approx\; \underline{x}(\xi,\eta) \;+\; \frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial \xi}\,\Delta\xi \;=\; \underline{x} + \underline{t}\,\Delta\xi , \]

so the physical displacement is the vector \(\underline{t}\,\Delta\xi\). Its length is \(\|\underline{t}\|\,\Delta\xi\). That is the entire derivation: \(\|\underline{t}\|\) is how much physical boundary you get per unit of parent boundary, at that point on the face.

The figure below makes the point by taking the same parent step on two different faces.

The same Δη on two faces. On the left both marked intervals are identical: the parent domain has no idea which face is which. On the right the inner one has become a short piece of arc and the outer one a piece twice as long, because ∥t∥ on the outer face is exactly twice its value on the inner one (the figure prints both). That single number is the whole difference, and it is why the outer arc is twice the length of the inner one.

Three consequences worth stating out loud:

  • \(\|\underline{t}\|\) is a local stretch factor.
  • It varies along the face whenever the mapping is not affine. You cannot pull it out of the integral the way you can a constant element length in 1D.
  • Only the length matters. \(\underline{t}\) also tells you which way the face runs, but the direction cancels out of \(d\Gamma\); it comes back later, when you need the outward normal for a flux.

20.2.2 Why it is not \(\det(DF)\)

\(\det(DF)\) is the answer to a different question. It asks: given a small rectangle \(\Delta\xi \times \Delta\eta\) in the parent domain, what is the area of its image? That image is a parallelogram spanned by both columns of \(DF\), and its area is \(|\det(DF)|\,\Delta\xi\,\Delta\eta\).

On a face you have no second direction to span anything with. You are not carrying a rectangle across the mapping, you are carrying a segment. One column, not two; a length, not an area.

Two different objects. Inside the element, both columns of DF are in play and they span a parallelogram — that area is what det DF measures. On the face only one column survives, and what you need is the length of that single arrow. They are not the same quantity and they do not even have the same units: one is a length per unit parent length, the other an area per unit parent area.

One more reason to be careful: on an axis-aligned rectangle the two are proportional. For an \(h_1 \times h_2\) element, \(DF\) is diagonal, \(\det(DF) = h_1h_2/4\), and on the \(\eta\)-face \(\|\underline{t}\| = h_1/2\). Their ratio is \(h_2/2\) — a constant. Use the wrong one on a rectangular mesh and every face integral is off by the same factor, which looks far more like a units slip than a bug, and does not disturb the convergence rate at all. It only becomes obvious once an element is curved or distorted, which is exactly the case the figure above shows.

20.3 Summary

  • 2D Quadrature is the Product of 1D Rules. 1D rules are established in each of the directions, coordinates are the union of individual 1D coordinates, and quadrature weights are the product of 1D quadrature weights. This is a similar story to how 2D tensor product basis functions were defined in Class 11.
  • A face integral is a mapped 1D operation. Hold one parent coordinate at \(\pm 1\), let the other vary, and reuse the same Gauss points you already have in 1D with the additional 2D coordinate.
  • The Jacobian on a face is \(\|\underline{t}\|\), not \(\det(DF)\), and \(\underline{t}\) is one column of \(DF\) corresponding to the derivative with respect to whichever parent coordinate is free.
  • A basis function contributes to a face only if its node is on it. Everything else integrates to zero there, which is why the element’s face vector has only \(p+1\) non-zero entries.
  • The widget’s lengths miss the true \(\pi/2\) and \(\pi\) by about half a percent, and adding quadrature points does not help. It is tempting to blame the quadrature, but that is not it: the rule converges perfectly well, settling on \(1.5624171\) by about eight points and staying there. What it is converging to is the length of the element’s own edge, which is a biquadratic curve through three points on the circle, not the circle. The half percent is geometry error, not integration error, and the only cures are a finer mesh or a higher-degree geometry.
