17  Class 17: Convergence Rates

How fast the error goes away, and how we measure it

We have built a finite element solver and we have looked at its solution and its derivative. The obvious next question is whether the answer is any good, and that question has a surprisingly precise answer: not what the error is, but how fast it goes away as we refine the mesh.

17.1 What we measure the error in

The error is a function, not a number:

\[ e(x) := u(x) - u^h(x) . \]

To get a number out of it we need a norm, and we need one that can ask about derivatives, because we already know the derivative is approximated particularly poorly. The Sobolev norms do exactly that. In one dimension:

\[ \|e\|_{H^0(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 \, dx, \qquad \|e\|_{H^1(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 + \left(e_{,x}\right)^2 dx, \qquad \|e\|_{H^2(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 + \left(e_{,x}\right)^2 + \left(e_{,xx}\right)^2 dx . \]

Each one includes everything the previous one had, plus one more derivative. \(H^0\) is the same thing as \(L^2\) — it only asks whether the values are close. \(H^1\) also asks whether the slopes are close. \(H^2\) also asks about curvature.

In several dimensions the same thing, written with the gradient:

\[ \|e\|_{H^0(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 \, d\Omega, \qquad \|e\|_{H^1(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 + \nabla e \cdot \nabla e \; d\Omega, \qquad \|e\|_{H^2(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 + \nabla e \cdot \nabla e + \nabla^2 e : \nabla^2 e \; d\Omega , \]

or in index notation, which is often easier to implement from:

\[ \|e\|_{H^0}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 \, d\Omega, \qquad \|e\|_{H^1}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 + e_{,i}\,e_{,i} \; d\Omega, \qquad \|e\|_{H^2}^2 = \int_\Omega e^2 + e_{,i}\,e_{,i} + e_{,ij}\,e_{,ij} \; d\Omega . \]

Repeated indices are summed, so \(e_{,i}e_{,i}\) in two dimensions is \(\left(e_{,x}\right)^2 + \left(e_{,y}\right)^2\), which is exactly \(\nabla e \cdot \nabla e\).

In practice we almost always report \(H^0\) and \(H^1\). \(H^0\) tells you whether the solution is right, while \(H^1\) tells you whether the fluxes and strains you extract from it are right, and those are usually the quantities of interest. \(H^2\) is not particularly usefule for the Poisson equation, but plays a bigger role in beam and shell problems and in mixing theory.

17.2 What \(h\) means

Every element has a size. The one the estimate uses is the diameter of the element — the longest distance between any two of its points — and then \(h\) is the largest of those over the whole mesh:

\[ h_K = \mathrm{diam}\left(\Omega^K\right) = \max_{\underline{a},\underline{b}\,\in\,\Omega^K} \left|\underline{a}-\underline{b}\right| , \qquad h = \max_K h_K . \]

In one dimension that is just the length of the interval. In two and three dimensions it is a diagonal, not an edge — which matters, because a long thin element has a much larger diameter than its short edge suggests.

h is the diameter, not an edge length. In 2D and 3D it is the longest diagonal across the element.The mesh's h is the largest of them. Note the stretched element: its short edge is small, but its diameter — and therefore its h — is not.

17.2.1 Quasi-uniformity is a property of a family of meshes

The estimates come with a hidden assumption, and it is easy to state imprecisely. A family of meshes \(\{\mathcal{T}_h\}\), indexed by \(h \to 0\), is quasi-uniform if there is a constant \(\rho > 0\) that does not depend on \(h\) such that

\[ \min_K h_K \;\geq\; \rho \, h \qquad \text{for every mesh in the family.} \]

The whole content is in does not depend on \(h\). Any single mesh has finitely many elements, so \(h/h_{\min}\) is automatically some finite number — you can always find a constant that works. You cannot look at one mesh and say whether it is quasi-uniform. The question is whether that ratio stays bounded as you refine, or grows without limit.

You have to watch a sequence, not a mesh. Top: uniform refinement, where the ratio of largest to smallest element stays at 1 forever. Bottom: graded refinement toward the left end, where the ratio grows at every level and is unbounded over the family. The bottom family is not quasi-uniform. The particular error estimators given here assume a quasi-uniform refinement, but we will see that in some cases not having such a quasi-uniform refinement may be good. This goes to show that these a-priori error estimators are just one resource that we have to improve accuracy.

So why does the assumption appear at all? Because it is what lets us write the estimate in terms of a single number \(h\). The underlying approximation results are local — they hold element by element in terms of \(h_K\), and need only shape regularity, a bound on how stretched any individual element may be. Quasi-uniformity is the extra condition that lets us replace every \(h_K\) by one global \(h\) and still have a sharp statement. The error constant \(C\) that we will see later degrades as \(h/h_{\min}\) grows.

17.2.2 Refining in the right place beats refining everywhere

A non-quasi-uniform family for mesh refinement is not always bad. If the solution is hard to resolve in one small region, putting your elements there is far more effective than spreading them evenly — and the a-priori estimate below, written for a single \(h\), simply does not describe what you get.

Here is the demonstration. Take

\[ u(x) = \tanh\!\big(25(x-\tfrac{1}{2})\big), \qquad f = -u_{,xx} , \]

which is perfectly smooth — it has derivatives of every order — but swings from \(-1\) to \(1\) across a narrow layer in the middle and is nearly flat everywhere else. Use quadratic elements, \(k=2\). Refine three ways, always to the same number of elements.

Why that region and not another. The top panel is the exact solution. Almost all of its variation happens inside the shaded strip — outside it, u is within a hair of ±1 and a single element would reproduce it almost exactly. Three things to take from this.

This has nothing to do with smoothness. \(\tanh\) is infinitely differentiable, so the solution is as regular as anyone could ask. The only thing that differs between the three rows is where the elements sit. Resolution and regularity are separate issues, and this is the resolution one.

Refining where it matters gives the least amount of error. Clustering at the layer reaches 2.91 — essentially the rate quadratic elements are capable of — and at 128 elements it is six times more accurate than uniform for exactly the same cost. That family is deliberately not quasi-uniform.

Refining in the wrong place doesn’t help. Clustering at the ends means that you don’t refine in the one region that needed elements. Its measured rate is 0.87, and at 128 elements it is still a thousand times less accurate than clustering at the layer. Putting elements somewhere is not the same as putting them somewhere useful.

Note also that the uniform row reads 2.60 rather than 3. The estimate is asymptotic, and on these meshes the layer is still only a few elements wide, so we are not yet in the range where it applies. That is a different effect again, and we return to it later.

So when we write \(\|e\| \leq C h^\beta \|u\|\) in the next section and talk about measuring a slope, understand what it describes: uniform refinement of a quasi-uniform family. That is the setting where a single \(h\) means something. Adaptive and graded meshes are common and are frequently better. Deciding where to put the elements needs different tools, a-posteriori estimators, which we are not covering here.

17.3 A-priori error estimates

A priori means before the fact: before you solve anything, you can say how the error will behave, purely from the polynomial degree, the mesh size, and how smooth the exact solution is. You do not need the computed answer to write it down. (The contrast is an a-posteriori estimate, which is computed after you solve, out of the solution you got, and which can give you an actual number and tell you which elements to refine. We are not doing those here.)

The standard a-priori estimate, for a sufficiently smooth exact solution, is

\[ \boxed{\;\|e\|_{H^s(\Omega)} \;\leq\; C \, h^{\beta} \, \|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Omega)}, \qquad \beta = \min\big(k+1-s,\; 2(k+1-m)\big)\;} \]

with \(k\) the polynomial degree, \(s\) the norm we measure in, and \(m\) the number of derivatives on a single term of the weak form. For the problems we solve in this class, \(m=1\), and the second argument is never lower, so in practice

\[ \beta = k + 1 - s . \]

17.3.1 When the solution is not smooth enough

If you look at the right hand side of the above boxed equation carefully, you will see that it contains \(\|u\|_{H^{k+1}}\). The estimate is only meaningful if that quantity is finite — that is, if the exact solution actually lives in \(H^{k+1}(\Omega)\). If it does not, the bound is vacuous as written, and the correct statement replaces \(k+1\) by however much regularity the solution really has.

If \(u \in H^{r}(\Omega)\) and \(r < k+1\), then

\[ \boxed{\;\|e\|_{H^s(\Omega)} \;\leq\; C \, h^{\min(k+1,\,r)-s} \, \|u\|_{H^{r}(\Omega)}\;} \]

so the rate is governed by the solution, not by the degree, and the norm bounding it is the one the solution actually belongs to. Once \(r\) drops below \(k+1\), using higher more polynomial degree does not improve the a priori error estimate because \(\min(k+1,r)\) stops moving.

This is not the \(\min\) in the earlier formula doing something. That one compares the approximation space against the variational form, and both of its arguments grow with \(k\). This is a third, separate limiter sitting outside that estimate entirely.

A worked example: suppose the exact solution happens to lie in \(H^{2}\), and we discretise it with quadratic elements, \(k=2\). Then \(\min(k+1, r) = \min(3, 2) = 2\), so

\[ \|e\|_{L^2} \leq C\,h^{2}\|u\|_{H^2}, \qquad \|e\|_{H^1} \leq C\,h^{1}\|u\|_{H^2} , \]

rather than the \(h^3\) and \(h^2\) that quadratic elements deliver on a smooth solution. One order has been lost in each norm, and switching to cubic elements would not recover it — \(\min(4,2)\) is still 2.

It is worth being clear in advance that it is a different mechanism from anything in Section 2.2: there, the solution was perfectly smooth and only the mesh was badly placed. Here the mesh may be flawless and the solution itself is the limit.

17.3.2 What the constant is

The constant in this above error estimate, \(C,\) is typically nebulous. While we don’t know exactly what it is, we do know that it is independent of:

  • the mesh size \(h\), so we can talk about the rate of error decrease, and
  • the exact solution \(u\), because the complexity of the error in from the solution has been factored into \(\|u\|_{H^{k+1}}\).

It depends on: the domain \(\Omega\), the polynomial degree \(k\), the shape-regularity and quasi-uniformity constants of the mesh family, and the coefficients of the problem (for us, \(\underline{\underline{\kappa}}\), through how strongly elliptic the operator is).

So the estimate tells you the slope of the error curve and not its height. You cannot use it to predict that your error will be \(10^{-4}\). You can use it to predict that halving \(h\) will divide the error by \(2^\beta\), and doing this in code tells you if your software is correct.

17.4 Turning the estimate into a straight line

The estimate is a power law, which is awkward to look at. Take a logarithm of both sides:

\[ \log \|e\|_{H^s} \;\leq\; \log\left(C\,\|u\|_{H^{k+1}}\right) + \beta \log h . \]

Everything in the first term on the right is a constant — it does not change as we refine. So if we plot \(\log\|e\|\) against \(\log h\), we should see a straight line of slope \(\beta\), sitting at a height we do not get to predict:

\[ \underbrace{\log\|e\|}_{y} = \beta \underbrace{\log h}_{x} + \underbrace{\log(C\|u\|)}_{\text{intercept}} . \]

Consequently, convergence results typically always shown on log–log axes. The quantity we care about is a slope, and only these axes make it a slope.

17.4.1 Dyadic refinement

To measure that slope we typically refine the mesh dyadically, where each level halves the element size, so \(h_{i+1} = h_i/2\). In one dimension that means splitting every element in two; in two dimensions, splitting every element into four.

Each level halves h. In 1D the element count doubles; in 2D it quadruples, because each element splits in both directions. The element size halves either way, which is what the estimate is written in terms of. Note what this costs: in 2D, three levels of refinement is 64 times as many elements.

Because each level halves \(h\), the slope between two consecutive levels has a very simple form. Starting from

\[ \boxed{\;\beta \approx \frac{\log\|e_{i+1}\| - \log\|e_{i}\|}{\log h_{i+1} - \log h_{i}}\;} \]

and substituting \(h_{i+1} = h_i/2\), the denominator is just \(-\log 2\), so

\[ \beta \approx \log_2\!\left(\frac{\|e_i\|}{\|e_{i+1}\|}\right)\]

In words: halve the mesh, and the rate is the base-2 logarithm of how many times smaller the error got. If the error drops by a factor of 4, the rate is 2. By 8, the rate is 3.

And the measured values are clean integers, which is exactly why this is such a good test of whether your code is right: a bug in assembly usually shows up as a rate of 1.4 or 0.9 rather than as an obviously wrong picture.

One more caution when using higher degree polynomials: The error cannot fall below what double precision can represent, so once it reaches roughly \(10^{-13}\) the curve flattens and the measured rate collapses — not because the method stopped working or is not accurate, but because the error estimate cannot capture it. On this same problem at \(k=5\), the rate holds at 6.00 out to 16 elements and then reads 4.94 at 32, where the error is already \(8.6\times10^{-13}\). If a high-degree run looks like it has stopped converging, check the size of the error before concluding that something is broken.

17.5 Actually computing the error norm

The norms above are integrals over \(\Omega\), and we evaluate them the same way we evaluate every other integral in this course: break the domain into elements, push each element back to the parent domain, and apply Gaussian quadrature.

Step 1 — split the global integral into element integrals.

\[ \|e\|_{H^0(\Omega)}^2 = \int_\Omega \left(u - u^h\right)^2 d\Omega = \sum_{e} \int_{\Omega^e} \left(u - u^h\right)^2 d\Omega . \]

Step 2 — change variables to the parent domain. Each element integral becomes an integral over the parent element, with the Jacobian determinant accounting for the change of area (or length):

\[ \int_{\Omega^e} \left(u - u^h\right)^2 d\Omega = \int_{\square} \left(u\!\left(\underline{x}^e(\underline{\xi})\right) - u^h(\underline{\xi})\right)^2 J^e(\underline{\xi}) \; d\square , \qquad J^e = \det\left(DF^e\right) . \]

Step 3 — apply quadrature.

\[ \boxed{\;\|e\|_{H^0(\Omega)}^2 \approx \sum_e \sum_q w_q \left(u\!\left(\underline{x}^e(\underline{\xi}_q)\right) - u^h(\underline{\xi}_q)\right)^2 J^e(\underline{\xi}_q)\;} \]

For the \(H^1\) norm, add the gradient term, which also uses \(DF^{-T}\).

\[ \|e\|_{H^1(\Omega)}^2 \approx \sum_e \sum_q w_q \left[ \left(u - u^h\right)^2 + \left(\nabla_{\underline{x}} u - DF^{-T}\nabla_{\underline{\xi}} u^h\right)\cdot \left(\nabla_{\underline{x}} u - DF^{-T}\nabla_{\underline{\xi}} u^h\right) \right] J^e \; . \]

17.5.1 The two solutions are evaluated differently

Inside that sum there are two things being evaluated at the same quadrature point, and they are reached by different routes.

The approximate solution is evaluated directly in the reference domain. It is built from local basis functions, and the IEN array tells us which coefficients to use:

\[ u^h(\underline{\xi}_q) = \sum_{a=0}^{n_{en}-1} d_{\,\mathrm{IEN}(a,e)} \; N_a(\underline{\xi}_q) . \]

No mapping is needed. The basis functions are defined on the parent element, so \(\xi_q\) is already the right input.

The exact solution must be evaluated at the physical point. \(u\) is a function of \(\underline{x}\), so we first have to find where this quadrature point actually sits in space, using the spatial mapping:

\[ \underline{x}^e(\underline{\xi}_q) = \sum_{a=0}^{n_{en}-1} \underline{x}_a \, N_a(\underline{\xi}_q), \qquad\text{then evaluate } u\!\left(\underline{x}^e(\underline{\xi}_q)\right) . \]

This is the same two-step move you already made when assembling the local force vector, where \(f\) had to be evaluated at \(\underline{x}(\underline{\xi}_q)\) rather than at \(\underline{\xi}_q\). If you mix the two up — feeding \(\xi_q\) straight into \(u\) — the code runs, the error comes out finite, and the convergence rate is wrong.

17.5.2 A practical warning about quadrature

The error \(e = u - u^h\) is not a polynomial, because \(u\) generally is not one. So the quadrature rule that was exact for your stiffness matrix is not exact here, and if you use too few points you end up measuring your quadrature error instead of your discretization error. Use more points than you think you need and confirm that adding points does not change the answer.
