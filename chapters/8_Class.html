8  Class 8: Assembly, Sparsity, and the Road to Quadrature

We now know

\[ (\mathrm{S}) \Longleftrightarrow (\mathrm{W}) \;\xRightarrow{\text{approximate}}\; (\mathrm{G}) \;\Longrightarrow\; (\mathrm{M}) \]

where \((\mathrm{M})\) is: given \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\), \(g : \Gamma_g \to \mathbb{R}\), \(h : \Gamma_h \to \mathbb{R}\), \(\underline{\underline{K}} : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{n_{sd}\times n_{sd}}\), find \(\underline{d}\) such that \(\underline{\underline{K}}\,\underline{d} = \underline{F}\), where

\[ K_{AB} = \int_\Omega \nabla N_A \cdot \underline{\underline{K}}\nabla N_B\;d\Omega \qquad\qquad F_A = \int_\Omega N_A f\;d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma_h} N_A h\;d\Gamma - \int_\Omega \nabla N_A \cdot \underline{\underline{K}} \left(\sum_{B \in \Gamma_g} g_B N_B\right) d\Omega \]

For a given problem, this becomes a matrix equation with \(n_{unk}\) rows and \(n_{unk}\) columns (note the symmetry — this will be shown to be sparse).

9 A one-dimensional example

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \int_0^L N_{0,x}N_{0,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{0,x}N_{1,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{0,x}N_{2,x}\,dx \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{0,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{1,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{2,x}\,dx \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{0,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{1,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{2,x}\,dx \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} d_0 \\ d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \int_0^L N_0 f\,dx + N_0(0)h - g\int_0^L N_{0,x}N_{3,x}\,dx \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_1 f\,dx - g\int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{3,x}\,dx \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_2 f\,dx - g\int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{3,x}\,dx \end{bmatrix} \]

9.1 Simplifying based on the supports of the functions

\(N_0\) and \(N_2\) are not adjacent — their supports (elements 0–1 and 1–2 respectively) share at most a single point, so \(\int_\Omega N_{0,x}N_{2,x}\,d\Omega = 0\). The same holds for \(N_0\)/\(N_3\) and \(N_1\)/\(N_3\). This is the origin of sparsity: most entries of \(K_{AB}\) vanish because most pairs of basis functions don’t overlap.

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \int_0^L N_{0,x}N_{0,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{0,x}N_{1,x}\,dx & 0 \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{0,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{1,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{1,x}N_{2,x}\,dx \\[4pt] 0 & \int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{1,x}\,dx & \int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{2,x}\,dx \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} d_0 \\ d_1 \\ d_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \int_0^L N_0 f\,dx + h \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_1 f\,dx \\[4pt] \int_0^L N_2 f\,dx - g\int_0^L N_{2,x}N_{3,x}\,dx \end{bmatrix} \]

Notice that all the integrals in \(\underline{\underline{K}}\) look the same, in some way:

  • Dirichlet (known) boundary-condition data looks the same as the integrals in \(\underline{\underline{K}}\) — it is just \(\int \nabla N_A \cdot \underline{\underline{K}} \nabla N_B\) against a known coefficient \(g_B\).
  • Neumann (known-flux) boundary-condition data is a number in 1D — but a curve in 2D and a surface in 3D.
  • Integrals with forcing functions on the right-hand side all look the same as each other.

That observation is what makes a general-purpose code possible: there are only a handful of distinct integral shapes to implement, no matter the problem or the dimension.

10 The local view: appearance on a single element

We want to solve integrals like

\[ \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} N_{i,x}\,N_{i+1,x}\;dx = \int_{x_0^e}^{x_1^e} N_{0,x}^e\,N_{1,x}^e\;dx \]

and similarly \(\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} N_{i+s,x}N_{i+t,x}\,dx = \int_{x_0^e}^{x_1^e} N_{s,x}^e N_{t,x}^e\,dx\) for \(s,t \in \{0,1\}\): the integral only depends on the local shape of the basis functions restricted to that one element, not on which global element it happens to be. This is why we can compute a small “element” integral once and reuse the recipe everywhere. Likewise for load terms:

\[ \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} N_i\,f\;dx = \int_{x_0^e}^{x_1^e} N_0^e\,f\;dx \qquad\qquad \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}} N_{i+1}\,f\;dx = \int_{x_0^e}^{x_1^e} N_1^e\,f\;dx \]

11 Numerical quadrature

Let \(p(x)\) be a function. How can we solve \(\int_a^b p\;dx\) in general on a computer? \(\to\) numerical quadrature.

Example: Riemann sums. Sum a bunch of bins with height \(h_i\), base length \(B_i\): \(\sum_i h_i B_i \approx\) area/integral, where \(h_i = p(x_i)\).

We want to pick some ideal choice of quadrature rule for polynomial basis functions:

\[ \int_a^b p(x)\;dx = \sum_{g=0}^{n_g - 1} w_g\,p(x_g) \]

for some polynomial degree, where \(w_g\) and \(x_g\) are special points and weights — the subject of the next class.
