13  Class 13: The Jacobian Matrix

The derivative of the spatial mapping

In Class 12 the spatial mapping told us where a parametric point goes:

\[ \underline{x}^e(\underline{\xi}) = \sum_{A=0}^{n_{en}-1} \underline{x}_A^e\,N_A(\underline{\xi}). \]

It does not say how much a small piece of the parent domain is stretched or turned on the way. We need that to change an integral over \(\Omega^e\) into an integral over the parent domain, and we need it to tell whether the element is usable at all. Both come from the derivative of the map.

13.1 The deformation gradient

Differentiating the sum gives the deformation gradient, also called the Jacobian matrix. Symbolically,

\[ DF = \frac{\partial \underline{x}}{\partial \underline{\xi}} = \sum_{A=0}^{n_{en}-1} \underline{x}_A^e \left(\nabla_\xi N_A\right)^T . \]

In index notation, with \(i\) running over spatial directions and \(j\) over parametric directions,

\[ \left(DF\right)_{ij} = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial \xi_j} = \sum_{A=0}^{n_{en}-1} x^e_{iA}\,\frac{\partial N_A}{\partial \xi_j}. \]

In matrix form in 2D, with \(\underline{\xi} = (\xi,\eta)\),

\[ DF = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial \xi} & \dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial \eta} \\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial \xi} & \dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial \eta} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle\sum_A x_A \dfrac{\partial N_A}{\partial \xi} & \displaystyle\sum_A x_A \dfrac{\partial N_A}{\partial \eta} \\[14pt] \displaystyle\sum_A y_A \dfrac{\partial N_A}{\partial \xi} & \displaystyle\sum_A y_A \dfrac{\partial N_A}{\partial \eta} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Two things to notice. The nodal coordinates \(\underline{x}_A^e\) are constants, so the only thing being differentiated is the basis functions: \(DF\) is built from \(\partial N_A/\partial \xi_j\), which is Homework 6 work. And \(DF\) depends on \(\underline{\xi}\), so unless the element is a parallelogram it is a different matrix at every point.

The determinant is the Jacobian,

\[ J = \det DF = \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial \xi}\frac{\partial x_2}{\partial \eta} - \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial \eta}\frac{\partial x_2}{\partial \xi}, \qquad d\Omega = J\,d\xi\,d\eta, \]

which is what lets every element integral be computed on the parent domain:

\[ \int_{\Omega^e} (\cdot)\,d\Omega = \int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\int_{-1}^{1} (\cdot)\;J(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta . \]

13.2 What the columns are

The first column of \(DF\) is \(\partial\underline{x}/\partial\xi\) and the second is \(\partial\underline{x}/\partial\eta\). These are tangent vectors: column 1 points along the image of a line of constant \(\eta\), column 2 along the image of a line of constant \(\xi\). Those are the curves already drawn on the Class 12 viewer.

Taken on their own, two arrows are not very informative. They become informative when you see what they are the edges of. Take a small square of side \(h\) in the parent domain. Its image under the map is a slightly curved patch. The parallelogram spanned by \(h\) times the two columns of \(DF\) is the flat approximation of that patch, and

\[ \text{area of the parallelogram} = |J|\,h^2 . \]

As \(h\) shrinks the curved patch and the parallelogram become the same shape. That is the statement that \(DF\) is the derivative. The sign of \(J\) says whether the image keeps the orientation of the original square or reverses it.

13.3 The map and its derivative, interactively

Same element as Class 12: a quarter annulus, \(1 \le r \le 2\), with nodes at the parametric node locations.

  • Drag the dot in the parent domain, or type \(\xi\) and \(\eta\). Both panels follow.
  • Drag the gold control points in the physical panel, or edit the table.

There are three separate things drawn, and it is worth keeping them apart:

  • The mapped grid is the faint blue and orange curves covering the whole element. These are the images of lines of constant \(\eta\) and constant \(\xi\), the same curves as in Class 12. They describe the map, not its derivative.
  • The gold square of side \(h\) sits in the parent domain around the current point, and its image is the filled patch in the physical panel. This is the actual image: every point of the square’s boundary put through \(\underline{x}^e(\underline{\xi})\), so its edges are curved.
  • The parallelogram is the dashed outline in the physical panel, built from \(h\) times the two columns of \(DF\). It is the flat approximation to that patch.

The gold square and its image each carry two arrowheads showing which way the boundary is traversed. On the gold square they always run counter-clockwise. If the arrowheads on the image run the other way, the map has reversed orientation there, which is \(J < 0\).

Shading is the value of \(J\), blue where positive and red where negative. Where \(J\) changes sign the black \(J = 0\) curve is drawn in both panels.

13.4 Reading the output

The table holds the ingredients. Columns \(\partial N_A/\partial\xi\) and \(\partial N_A/\partial\eta\) are the two components of \(\nabla_\xi N_A\), and the footer row is each of them weighted by the nodal coordinates, which is to say the two columns of \(DF\). Nothing in the matrix comes from anywhere else.

Under \(J = \det DF\) the three areas are printed together:

  • \(h^2\), the area of the square in the parent domain;
  • \(J h^2\), the area of the parallelogram \(DF\) predicts;
  • the signed area of the image of that square.

Shrink \(h\) and the ratio of the last two goes to \(1\). That limit is the definition of the derivative, and it is the reason \(d\Omega = J\,d\xi\,d\eta\) is allowed inside an integral.

13.5 Things to try

  1. Move the point around the default element. \(J\) changes from place to place: the quarter annulus stretches more at the outer radius than the inner one. A single number does not describe the whole element.
  2. Set \(h = 1.2\) and compare the filled patch with the dashed parallelogram. They differ. Now drag \(h\) down to \(0.05\) and they coincide. \(DF\) is a local statement.
  3. Set \(p_\xi = p_\eta = 1\). The four-node element is a bilinear map, and \(J\) is not constant: the shading still varies. A bilinear element is only a pure stretch when it is a parallelogram.
  4. Drag control point \(A = 1\) toward the interior of the element, past its neighbours, until part of the shading turns red. The \(J = 0\) curve appears, the image of the square flips over, and its arrowheads run opposite to the ones on the gold square. That is the 2D version of \(dx^e/d\xi < 0\) from Homework 7, case 4.
  5. Put the point on the \(J = 0\) curve. The parallelogram collapses to a line: the two columns of \(DF\) have become parallel, so the map squashes a 2D neighbourhood onto a 1D one and \(DF\) has no inverse. Derivatives with respect to \(x\), which need \(DF^{-1}\), are undefined there.
  6. Swap two control points that are not neighbours, for instance give \(A = 1\) the coordinates of \(A = 3\) and vice versa. The element outline may still look reasonable while the shading is entirely red. The outline does not tell you whether an element is valid; the sign of \(J\) does.

13.6 Derivatives with respect to \(x\)

The weak form needs \(\nabla_x N_A\), the gradient in physical coordinates. But \(N_A\) is defined on the parent domain, so the only derivatives we can take directly are \(\partial N_A/\partial \xi_j\). The chain rule connects them. Since \(N_A\) reaches \(\underline{\xi}\) only through \(\underline{x}\),

\[ \frac{\partial N_A}{\partial \xi_j} = \frac{\partial N_A}{\partial x_i}\,\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial \xi_j} = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial \xi_j}\,\frac{\partial N_A}{\partial x_i}, \]

which in matrix form is \(\nabla_\xi N_A = \left(DF\right)^T \nabla_x N_A\). Solving for the part we want,

\[ \boxed{\;\nabla_x N_A = \left(DF\right)^{-T}\,\nabla_\xi N_A\;} \]

In 2D the inverse is written out easily, since

\[ DF^{-1} = \frac{1}{J} \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial \eta} & -\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial \eta} \\[10pt] -\dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial \xi} & \dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial \xi} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad DF^{-T} = \frac{1}{J} \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial \eta} & -\dfrac{\partial x_2}{\partial \xi} \\[10pt] -\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial \eta} & \dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial \xi} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Note the \(1/J\). Everything said above about \(J\) changing sign now has a second consequence: at \(J = 0\) the spatial gradient does not exist, and near \(J = 0\) it is large. An element that is merely folded gives wrong areas; an element that is folded also gives unusable derivatives, which is what actually enters the stiffness matrix.

In each panel the thin curve is the level set of \(N_A\) through the current point, and the arrow is the gradient, which is perpendicular to its own level set in its own domain. The two arrows generally point in different directions: \(DF^{-T}\) rotates and rescales, so a gradient that points one way in the parent domain need not point that way in the element.

Things to notice here:

  1. Move the point around with the default element. The angle between the two gradients is rarely zero, and it changes from place to place. \(DF^{-T}\) is a different matrix at every point.
  2. Pick a corner basis function and put the point near the opposite corner. Both gradients are nearly zero: that basis function has almost no influence there.
  3. Drag a control point until \(J\) gets small. The magnitude of \(\nabla_x N_A\) grows, because of the \(1/J\). The parametric gradient does not change at all, since it knows nothing about the element’s shape.
  4. Set \(p = 1\) and compare with \(p = 2\). The parametric gradients are simpler, but the spatial ones are still position-dependent, because \(DF^{-T}\) is.

13.7 Next

\(J\) is a polynomial in \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) whose degree depends on the element, and that degree is what decides how many quadrature points are enough. With \(J\) for the area change and \(DF^{-T}\) for the derivatives, every piece of the element stiffness matrix can now be written as an integral over the parent domain and evaluated by quadrature.
