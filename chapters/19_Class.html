What carries over from the 1D solver, and the two places it genuinely changes

The flowchart in Class 15 laid out the one-dimensional solver step by step. Going to two or three dimensions does not add steps to that chart, and it does not reorder them. The architecture is the same: build the arrays, loop the elements, loop the quadrature points, scatter, solve.

What changes is what lives inside some of the boxes. Most of them scale up without any new idea — a longer vector here, a bigger element matrix there. Three of them change in kind, and those three are what the next few classes are really about.

The column down the middle below is that shared infrastructure, drawn the same way as the trajectory in Class 15: the steps, the element loop, the quadrature loop. It is the same in any number of dimensions. Click any box and the one-dimensional version of that step appears on the left and the multidimensional version on the right, tagged as unchanged, merely larger, or genuinely different.

19.1 What the picture is telling you

Eight of the eleven steps are either identical or simply larger. You already know how to write them. Three are not, and they are worth naming before you meet them in code.

The Jacobian, and spatial gradients. These two go together. In one dimension the Jacobian is a single number and the chain rule is a division: \(dN/dx\) is \((dN/d\xi)\) over the Jacobian. In two dimensions it becomes \(\nabla_x N = (DF)^{-T}\,\nabla_\xi N\) — you invert a matrix and transpose it, at every quadrature point of every element. Using \(DF^{-1}\) instead of \(DF^{-T}\) is the single most common bug in a first 2D code, and on a mesh of rectangles it will not show up, because \(DF\) is then diagonal and the two agree. It only breaks once an element is distorted.

Neumann boundary terms. In one dimension the boundary of the domain is two points, and \(h\) enters as one number added to one entry of \(\mathbf{F}\). There is no integral. In two dimensions the boundary is a curve, and \(\int_{\Gamma_h} N_A\,h\;d\Gamma\) is a real integral that needs its own quadrature loop along each boundary face, with its own arc-length Jacobian — which is not \(\det DF\). That machinery has no one-dimensional counterpart at all, which is why it gets a class of its own.
