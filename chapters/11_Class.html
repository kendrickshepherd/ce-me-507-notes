11  Class 11: Tensor-Product Basis Functions in 2D

Building 2D Lagrange bases from 1D ones

A 2D Lagrange basis function on the parent square \([-1,1]^2\) is the product of one 1D basis function in \(\xi\) and one in \(\eta\):

\[ N_A(\xi,\eta) = N_a(\xi)\,N_b(\eta), \qquad A = a + (p_\xi+1)\,b . \]

The index \(\xi\) varies fastest. The interpolation points are equally spaced.

In the 3D plot:

  • Blue curve on the back wall (\(\eta = 1\)): the 1D factor \(N_a(\xi)\).
  • Orange curve on the side wall (\(\xi = -1\)): the 1D factor \(N_b(\eta)\).
  • Dashed slices: every cut through the surface is a 1D function times a constant, \(N_A(\xi,\eta_0) = N_b(\eta_0)\,N_a(\xi)\) and \(N_A(\xi_0,\eta) = N_a(\xi_0)\,N_b(\eta)\).
  • Nodes: \(N_A = 1\) at its own node (gold) and \(0\) at every other node (black).

Drag the 3D plot to rotate it; the view is kept when you change a slider. Hover over the surface or curves to read values.

Each panel shows every 1D basis function of that degree (grey), the active one (solid), and the 2D slice along that direction (dashed), which is the active one scaled by the printed constant.

11.2 Strides: from \((a,b)\) to \(A\)

Looking straight down on the element, the basis functions sit on a \((p_\xi+1)\times(p_\eta+1)\) grid of nodes. Each direction gets a stride, \(s_0 = 1\) and \(s_i = \prod_{j=0}^{i-1}(p_j+1)\), so

\[ A = \sum_i a_i\,s_i = a\cdot 1 + b\,(p_\xi+1)\;\big[\,+\;c\,(p_\xi+1)(p_\eta+1)\text{ in 3D}\big], \qquad a_i = (A \,/\!/\, s_i) \bmod (p_i+1). \]

A step in \(\xi\) changes \(A\) by 1; a step in \(\eta\) jumps a whole row, \(p_\xi+1\). The plot is the current \(N_A\) seen from above, with each node labelled by its \(A\). Click any node to select that basis function; the sliders and plots above follow.

11.3 Things to try

  1. Set \(p_\xi = p_\eta = 1\). Every basis function is a bilinear “tent corner”, and each slice is a straight line.
  2. Set \(p_\xi = p_\eta = 2\) and pick the center node \((a,b) = (1,1)\). This is the interior “bubble” function; it is zero on the whole boundary of the element.
  3. Pick an edge node such as \((a,b) = (1,0)\) and move \(\eta_0\) onto \(\eta = 0\) or \(\eta = 1\). The dashed \(\xi\)-slice collapses to zero, because \(N_0(\eta)\) vanishes at the other \(\eta\) nodes.
  4. Raise the degree to 5 or 6 and look at a basis function next to the boundary. The large oscillations in the 1D factors (the Runge effect) carry straight over into the 2D surface.
