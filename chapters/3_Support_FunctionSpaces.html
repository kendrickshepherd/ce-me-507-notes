Appendix A — Support: Norms and Function Spaces

Supporting material for Class 3, from a conversation at the board about where the integrals in \(L^2\) and \(H^1\) come from.

B From a discrete norm to an integral

For a finite list of numbers, the norm is the familiar one:

\[ \|\underline{u}\| = \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2 + \cdots + u_n^2} \qquad\qquad \|\underline{u}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i^2 \]

Now let those numbers be samples of a function, \(u_i = u(x_i)\). Adding more samples must not make the norm grow without bound, so each term carries a weight \(w_i\) — the piece of the domain it stands for:

\[ \|\underline{u}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i^2 \, w_i \qquad \xrightarrow[\;n \to \infty\;]{} \qquad \int_0^L u^2 \, dx \]

C The \(L^2\) (Lebesgue) norm

\[ u \in L^2(\Omega) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \int_\Omega u^2 \, d\Omega < \infty \qquad\qquad \|u\|_{L^2} = \left(\int_\Omega u^2 \, d\Omega\right)^{1/2} \]

Membership depends on the domain as much as on the formula. Since the test is on \(u^2\), compare two functions whose squares are the two classic borderline integrands:

\[ u(x) = \frac{1}{x} \;\Rightarrow\; u^2 = \frac{1}{x^2} \qquad\qquad v(x) = \frac{1}{x^{1/4}} \;\Rightarrow\; v^2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} \]

(\(v\) is the fourth root of \(1/x\); squaring it gives \(1/\sqrt{x}\).)

\(\Omega = (0,1)\) \(\Omega = (1,\infty)\)
\(u = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x^2} = \infty\) \(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^2} = 1\)
\(v = \dfrac{1}{x^{1/4}}\) \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}} = 2\) \(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}} = \infty\)

Reading those off as membership in \(H^0 = L^2\):

  • \(\dfrac{1}{x} \notin H^0\big((0,1)\big)\)   but   \(\dfrac{1}{x} \in H^0\big((1,\infty)\big)\)
  • \(\dfrac{1}{x^{1/4}} \in H^0\big((0,1)\big)\)   but   \(\dfrac{1}{x^{1/4}} \notin H^0\big((1,\infty)\big)\)

The two ends make opposite demands: near a singularity the function must not blow up too fast, while over an infinite tail it must decay fast enough. For a pure power \(u = x^{-p}\), so \(u^2 = x^{-2p}\),

\[ \int_0^1 x^{-2p}\,dx < \infty \iff p < \tfrac{1}{2} \qquad\qquad \int_1^\infty x^{-2p}\,dx < \infty \iff p > \tfrac{1}{2} \]

No single \(p\) satisfies both — so no pure power lies in \(L^2\big((0,\infty)\big)\), \(1/x\) included.

D Sobolev norms: \(H^0\) and \(H^1\)

\[ \underbrace{\|u\|_{H^0} = \left(\int_\Omega u^2\, d\Omega\right)^{1/2}}_{H^0 \;=\; L^2} \qquad\qquad \|u\|_{H^1} = \left(\int_\Omega u^2 + \left(u_{,x}\right)^2 d\Omega\right)^{1/2} \]

The extra derivative term is what makes \(H^1\) a measure of a function’s energy: two functions can be the same “size” in \(L^2\) while costing very different amounts to bend.

This is why the weak form of Class 3 is posed in \(H^1\): the integral \(\int_0^1 w_{,x}u_{,x}\,dx\) only makes sense for functions whose derivatives are square integrable.
