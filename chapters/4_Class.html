4  Class 4: Strong and Weak Forms of the Boundary Value Problem

5 Strong form of the boundary value problem (Poisson)

Given \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\), \(g : \Gamma_g \to \mathbb{R}\), and \(h : \Gamma_h \to \mathbb{R}\), find \(u : \bar\Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) such that

\[ -\nabla\cdot\left(\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right) = -\left(K_{ij}u_{,j}\right)_{,i} = f \qquad \text{in } \Omega \]

\[ u = g \qquad \text{on } \Gamma_g \]

\[ \left(\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right)\cdot\underline{n} = K_{ij}u_{,j}\,n_i = h \qquad \text{on } \Gamma_h \]

6 In one dimension

\[ -\left(K u_{,x}\right)_{,x} = f \qquad \text{in } \Omega \qquad\qquad u = g \qquad \text{on } \Gamma_g \qquad\qquad (Ku_{,x})\,n = h \qquad \text{on } \Gamma_h \]

The one-dimensional boundary \(\Gamma\) is just the two endpoints, so \(n\) can only be \(-1\) or \(+1\) — the outward normal at the left end always points in \(-x\), and at the right end always in \(+x\).

The Neumann boundary is wherever a flux \(h\) is prescribed instead of a value \(g\). At the left end, the outward normal is \(-1\); at the right end, it is \(+1\) — that sign is why the Neumann condition in one dimension is written \(-u_{,x}(0) = h\) but \(+u_{,x}(L) = h\): both are \((Ku_{,x})\cdot n = h\) once \(K=1\) is included and \(n\) is substituted in.

7 Coming from the exact solution

Last time, we found the exact solution to the 1D Poisson problem to be

\[ u(x) = g + (1-x)h + \int_x^1\left(\int_0^y f(z)\,dz\right)dy \]

So we’re done… why do anything more?

  • What if we don’t have a simple function \(f\)?
  • What if the domain is not one-dimensional?

Want to convert this into a representation we can compute on. Idea: use an inner product and function space to “describe” our function.

\[ \mathcal{S} = \left\{\, u \in H^1(\Omega) \;\middle|\; u(x) = g(x) \text{ for } x \in \Gamma_g \,\right\} \qquad \leftarrow \textbf{trial function space} \]

\[ \mathcal{V} = \left\{\, w \in H^1(\Omega) \;\middle|\; w(x) = 0 \text{ for } x \in \Gamma_g \,\right\} \qquad \leftarrow \textbf{test function space} \]

Recall \(H^1(\Omega) = \left\{ f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \;\middle|\; \int_\Omega f^2 + \nabla f \cdot \nabla f \; d\Omega < \infty \right\}\) — in 1D, these are continuous functions. (See the function-space appendix for more.)

8 Weak form of the boundary value problem

Given \(f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\), \(g : \Gamma_g \to \mathbb{R}\), \(h : \Gamma_h \to \mathbb{R}\), and \(\underline{\underline{K}} : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{n_{sd} \times n_{sd}}\) symmetric positive definite, find \(u \in \mathcal{S}\) such that for every \(w \in \mathcal{V}\),

\[ (\mathrm{W}) \qquad \int_\Omega \nabla w \cdot \underline{\underline{K}} \nabla u \; d\Omega = \int_\Omega wf\;d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma_h} wh\;d\Gamma \]

In one dimension, this is

\[ (\mathrm{W}) \qquad \int_0^L w_{,x}\,K\,u_{,x}\;dx = \int_0^L wf\;dx + w u_{,x}\Big\rvert_{x=0}^{x=L} \qquad \text{or, in our case,} \qquad \int_0^L w_{,x}\,K\,u_{,x}\;dx = \int_0^1 wf\;dx + w(0)\,h \]

9 What does this mean?

Example: put an object in a bag, try to guess the object.

  • → bag size is the “space” of objects you’re guessing in
  • → people can come look in the bag & describe with 1 word

(In class, a mousetrap toy.)

The words are what we use to “test” or “measure” our object against.

If we have all possible descriptors, we should know precisely what the object is. Otherwise, we pick a set of “best” words with which to describe.

Think about this — it is sort of the way we come to know God.

  • → descriptors, experiences, etc., but we have limited knowledge; at some point we will know — John 17:3

10 Formal proof of equivalence (strong \(\Rightarrow\) weak)

Start from the strong form and multiply by an arbitrary \(w\), then integrate over \(\Omega\):

\[ -\nabla\cdot\left(\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right) = f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad -\int_\Omega w\,\nabla\cdot\left(\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right)\,d\Omega = \int_\Omega wf\,d\Omega \qquad (w \text{ arbitrary}) \]

The left side needs the product rule, applied to \(\nabla\cdot(w\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u)\):

\[ \nabla\cdot\left(w\,\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right) = \nabla w\cdot\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u + w\,\nabla\cdot\left(\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right) \]

so that, integrating the left side and applying the Divergence Theorem,

\[ \int_\Omega \nabla\cdot\left(w\,\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right) d\Omega = \int_\Gamma \left(w\,\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right)\cdot \underline{n}\;d\Gamma \]

Combining these two facts, the term we wanted rearranges into

\[ -\left(\int_\Gamma \left(w\,\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right)\cdot\underline{n}\;d\Gamma - \int_\Omega \nabla w \cdot \underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u \;d\Omega\right) = \int_\Omega wf\,d\Omega \]

\[ \Longrightarrow \qquad \int_\Omega \nabla w \cdot \underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u \; d\Omega = \int_\Omega wf\,d\Omega + \int_\Gamma \left(w\,\underline{\underline{K}}\nabla u\right)\cdot\underline{n}\;d\Gamma \]

\[ \Longrightarrow \qquad \int_\Omega w_{,i}K_{ij}u_{,j}\;d\Omega = \int_\Omega wf\,d\Omega + \int_\Gamma w K_{ij}u_{,j}\,n_i\;d\Gamma \]

But \(w(x) = 0\) on \(\Gamma_g\), and \(\Gamma = \Gamma_g \cup \Gamma_h\), so the boundary integral only survives over \(\Gamma_h\) — where the strong form tells us \(K_{ij}u_{,j}n_i = h\):

\[ \boxed{\int_\Omega w_{,i}K_{ij}u_{,j}\;d\Omega = \int_\Omega wf\,d\Omega + \int_{\Gamma_h} wh\;d\Gamma} \]

which is exactly \((\mathrm{W})\). The next class continues the other direction — \((\mathrm{W}) \Rightarrow (\mathrm{S})\) — which is a genuinely different (and more delicate) argument.
